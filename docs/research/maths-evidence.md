# Teaching Year 3 (Stage 2, NSW) mathematics so every student reaches their potential

Evidence summary, compiled October 2026. Australian sources first; international sources are labelled **[International]** and used only where no Australian equivalent was found. Sources marked (verified) were fetched and checked while writing this report. Sources marked (not fetched) are well known, but their wording was not re-checked this time.

## How strong is the evidence? A quick key

- **Strong**: several meta-analyses or systematic reviews, consistent findings (e.g. retrieval and spacing practice, explicit teaching for new material, harms of fixed ability grouping, number lines for fractions).
- **Moderate**: good studies but fewer of them, or findings from older students extended down to Year 3 (e.g. worked examples, metacognition, CPA sequencing as a package).
- **Emerging or weak**: plausible and widely used, but with little rigorous outcome data (e.g. number talks, growth mindset interventions, most maths anxiety interventions, specific rounding pedagogy).

## The big picture for a mixed-ability Year 3 class

The spread is wide. Dianne Siemon reports that access to multiplicative thinking "largely explains the seven-year range" in achievement in the middle years (Siemon, MAV 2024, verified). A Year 3 class will contain students still counting by ones and students who reason multiplicatively. The evidence does not point to one method. It points to a combination:

1. **Explicit teaching of new ideas** with gradual release (I do, we do, you do), short chunks and frequent checks (CESE 2025; AERO 2026).
2. **Shared, challenging tasks with enabling and extending prompts**, so that everyone works on the same mathematics at different depths (Sullivan, Clarke and colleagues).
3. **Flexible, short-term small groups formed from assessment evidence**, not fixed ability groups (Siemon, Vic DE monograph; EEF).
4. **Spaced retrieval of facts and earlier content** every day (AERO 2021/2026).
5. **Talk that compares strategies and connects representations** (Attard 2014/2015; QCAA 2022).

The NSW syllabus framing matches this. Explicit teaching and Working Mathematically are not alternatives. CESE's own guidance says teachers can move between I do, we do and you do several times in one lesson, adjusting scaffolding to readiness (CESE 2025, verified).

## 1. Explicit teaching in maths

**What the Australian evidence says**

- *What Works Best 2025: Explicit teaching* (CESE, NSW DoE, verified) reports that explicit teaching improves outcomes, especially for new or complex material. NSW students who reported explicit teaching were 1.8 months ahead in Year 7 and 2.4 months ahead in Year 9. It is also linked to higher academic self-concept, *particularly for lower-achieving students*. The chapter describes I do, we do, you do as a gradual release that can loop several times within a lesson. It also explains the **expertise reversal effect**: low-knowledge learners need more guidance and high-knowledge learners need less.
- CESE also notes, for maths specifically, that fluency with basic facts frees working memory for problem solving. Fact fluency is "not a strict prerequisite for working mathematically" but improves efficiency.
- *At a glance: Explicit instruction* (AERO, July 2026, verified) lists I do, we do, you do, small chunks, regular checks for understanding, explaining why the task matters, and keeping lessons active through questioning and peer discussion.
- *What Works Best 2020* (CESE, verified) says explicit teaching means telling students why they are learning something, connecting to prior knowledge, saying what they will do and how, and saying what success looks like (learning intentions and success criteria).
- AERO's *Explicit instruction in maths* video and facilitation guide (Dec 2022) gives a maths-specific model for teacher PL.

**Worked examples and interleaving.** CESE 2025 does not cover worked examples or interleaving directly. AERO's spacing and retrieval guide does not cover interleaving either. The EEF **[International]** recommends studying worked examples, including incorrect ones, and comparing solution methods. Its own rating of the worked-example evidence for this age is weak to moderate, because most studies are with older students. Interleaving (mixing problem types in practice) has good laboratory support but few Year 3 classroom trials. **Evidence strength: strong for explicit teaching of new content; moderate for worked examples; emerging for interleaving at this age.**

**Classroom implications**

- Open with a 5 to 10 minute daily review of earlier content, not just yesterday's lesson.
- Model with a think-aloud ("I'm looking at the denominator first because it tells me the size of the part"). Then do a *we do* with mini whiteboards before any independent work.
- Use paired worked examples: one fully worked, then a near-identical one with a step missing for students to complete. Include an "Ash's mistake" example for error analysis.
- Release early the students who show they have it on the whiteboard check. They move to the extending prompt while you keep a small group for a second *we do* (this is the expertise reversal effect in practice).
- Mix earlier topics into practice sets (e.g. three fraction items, two money items, one angle item) once a skill is secure.

## 2. Concrete, pictorial, abstract (CPA), representations and manipulatives

**Evidence**

- The NSW 2022 syllabus names key representations: number lines, bar models, arrays, linear (length) models for fractions, area models for multiplication and so on (NSW DoE STEM 2023 workshop on mathematical tools and representations, verified). Stage 2 content repeatedly asks students to "model", "represent" and "record the method used" (e.g. "Model addition with and without regrouping and record the method used", Additive relations B, verified).
- The EEF *Improving Mathematics in KS2 and 3* **[International]** (verified) found the support for concrete manipulatives "stronger" (five meta-analyses) and for visual representations moderate (two WWC reviews). Its cautions are useful: every manipulative needs a clear purpose; manipulatives are a **temporary scaffold** removed when students show understanding (not at a set age); **number lines are particularly effective** in this age range; and too many representations at once can confuse.
- I found no Australian trial testing "CPA" as a named sequence. The underlying ideas (material, then image, then symbol, with explicit links between them) are consistent with the NSW syllabus and Siemon's Big Ideas. **Evidence strength: moderate to strong for using representations and materials well; weak for any rigid three-stage CPA sequence.**

**Classroom implications**

- Choose one or two representations per concept and stay with them (e.g. fraction strips and the number line for fractions; base-10 blocks then place value chart then algorithm for regrouping).
- Always connect the forms explicitly. Have students record the symbols *while* moving the materials ("I traded 1 ten for 10 ones, so I cross out the 4 and write 3").
- Let materials go when students no longer need them, and let students who need them keep them. This is a quiet, low-stigma way to differentiate.

## 3. Differentiation in mixed-ability classes

**Enabling and extending prompts.** Clarke, Roche, Cheeseman and Sullivan (2014, *Australian Mathematics Teacher*, verified) define an enabling prompt as one "for students who have difficulty making a start on the main task" and an extending prompt as one "for students who find the main task quite straightforward". After trialling these lessons, many more teachers named the prompts as their most important planning change. Challenging tasks are defined as tasks that allow sustained thinking, decision making and some risk-taking. Teachers' main shifts were holding back from telling, and giving more think time. Students sometimes pushed back by asking for simpler tasks. The recommended structure is a clear task launch, enough work time, clear recording expectations, and a whole-class review at the end. This maps onto the "Discuss and connect" phase. Peter Sullivan's book *Challenging Mathematical Tasks* (Oxford University Press, 2018, not fetched) expands the model.

**Low floor, high ceiling tasks.** The NSW DoE *Thinking Mathematically* resources (verified example: *Finding numbers on a number line, Stage 2 and 3*) show the model. Everyone works on one task (locate unmarked points on a 0 to 150 line). Paper strips for halving act as the enabler, and "find what zero is" when the line does not start at zero is the extender. Students must "prove" their answers.

**Ability grouping.** Siemon's monograph *Issues in the Teaching of Mathematics: Ability Grouping* (Vic DE, c. 2021, verified) is the strongest Australian synthesis:
- Between-class streaming shows essentially no achievement benefit (Slavin, 1990).
- Within-class grouping has small positive effects *only* when groups are formed around a precise shared skill.
- Fixed, long-term groups behave like streaming. They widen gaps the longer they run, and low groups get narrower curriculum and lower expectations.
- Low-group placement is linked to shame, disengagement and weaker maths identity. High groups can bring anxiety, especially for girls.
- Placement can reflect socioeconomic status or behaviour rather than prior achievement.

The EEF **[International]** reaches the same conclusion: setting does not raise attainment on average, slightly harms lower sets and widens gaps.

**Targeted teaching.** Siemon (MAV 2024, verified) describes targeted teaching around a few big ideas (place value, multiplicative thinking). It is "not ability grouping", and students should spend most maths time in mixed groups on rich tasks. Identifying and responding to needs in multiplicative thinking produced effect sizes of 0.4 to 1.9. "Not everything needs to be differentiated."

The EEF **[International]** adds that small-group intervention should be based on assessment, start early, use explicit and systematic teaching, link back to class lessons, and avoid pulling children out of essential or enjoyable learning.

**Evidence strength: strong on the harms of fixed grouping; moderate on enabling and extending prompts (rich classroom research, few controlled trials); moderate to strong for targeted, assessment-based small group teaching.**

**Classroom implications**

- Plan every main task with one enabling prompt (fewer or smaller numbers, a material, a partly-completed representation) and one extending prompt (generalise, reverse the problem, "find all possibilities", explain why).
- Give the enabling prompt only after students have genuinely tried, so they still experience productive struggle.
- Run "teacher table" groups of 10 to 15 minutes for students named by today's exit ticket or hinge question. Change the membership every lesson or every week.
- Do not label tables or groups by ability. Keep everyone on the same topic and the same learning intention.

## 4. Mathematical talk and reasoning

**Evidence**

- Attard's *Framework for Engagement with Mathematics* (Attard 2014; applied in NSWTF *Journal of Professional Learning*, 2015, verified) puts **substantive conversation** about maths first among the "pedagogical repertoires", alongside positive and challenging tasks, choice, relevance, variety and purposeful technology. Practical points: move beyond drill alone, include student reflection in every lesson, use open tasks so all students can experience success, and use talk to build and assess Working Mathematically.
- **Number talks** (QCAA factsheet, 2022, verified) last 5 to 15 minutes. A problem is written horizontally and solved mentally. All answers are recorded without comment, then students explain strategies while the teacher draws them (e.g. on an empty number line), and strategies are compared. Victoria's toolkit includes a Derrimut Primary School number talks case study (verified). **Evidence strength: weak to emerging.** The supporting sources (Parrish, Humphreys and Parker, Fosnot and Dolk) are practitioner books, not trials. Number talks are low cost and fit the syllabus emphasis on flexible mental strategies ("compare and evaluate strategies ... reasoning which strategy may be most efficient", Additive relations A, verified).
- The Victorian toolkit's 12 scaffolding practices (verified) include "Convince me" (asking for justification), Noticing, Probing and Reflecting/reviewing. These are a useful vocabulary for the Discuss and Connect phase.
- The EEF **[International]** supports self-explanation and teaching metacognition (planning, monitoring, evaluating) with teacher modelling first (moderate evidence, six meta-analyses).

**Discuss and connect.** The end-of-lesson review in the Sullivan and Clarke model (Clarke et al., 2014) is where the teacher chooses student work in a deliberate order (usually from the least to the most sophisticated strategy) and names the mathematics. This is the most important point in the lesson for students who used the enabling prompt to see where they are heading. It is also where students who used the extending prompt get to explain their thinking.

**Vocabulary.** Maths words in Year 3 often have everyday meanings that clash with their maths meanings (e.g. "volume" on the TV, "right" angle versus right-hand side, "change" as money versus as a difference). AERO's explainer *Developing maths proficiency* (2024, verified) notes that language and literacy influence maths proficiency. Teach key terms explicitly, show them on a word wall with a picture, and require full-sentence answers ("I know three-quarters is greater than two-thirds because ...").

**Classroom implications**

- Run a 10-minute number talk or "which one doesn't belong" 2 to 3 times a week as part of warm-ups.
- Use sentence stems: "I agree with ... because ...", "My strategy is like ...'s because ...", "I noticed ...".
- In Discuss and connect, show 2 or 3 pieces of student work and ask "How are these the same? Which is more efficient here? When would you use each?"

## 5. Fluency: number facts, spaced practice, low-stakes quizzing

**Evidence**

- AERO's *Spacing and retrieval practice guide* (2021, updated Sept 2026, verified): spread a concept across two or more lessons; review earlier content at lesson start; use cumulative quizzes; keep retrieval **low-stakes** and "challenging but not discouraging"; mix factual and higher-order questions; involve every student (e.g. **mini whiteboards**); and correct errors promptly. Cited evidence includes Adesope et al. (2017): "an overwhelming amount of evidence" links low-stakes practice testing to achievement. **Evidence strength: strong.**
- CESE 2025 (verified): fact fluency frees working memory.
- EEF **[International]**: build fluent recall of number facts and procedures *with* understanding, and teach choosing between strategies (strongest WWC-backed recommendations).
- Year 3 fact targets in NSW: "Recall multiplication facts for 2, 4, 5 and 10, and related division facts" (Multiplicative relations, Stage 2 Year A Unit 04, verified), plus addition and subtraction strategies such as bridging the decades, compensation and the complement principle (Additive relations A, verified).

**A note on speed.** The Victorian maths anxiety page does not mention timed tests, and there is no Australian trial showing timed drills cause anxiety. Given the anxiety evidence in section 7, the safer approach is to aim for accuracy and recall *without* public time pressure.

**Classroom implications**

- A 5-minute daily retrieval warm-up: 3 to 5 questions, one from last week, one from last month, one from last term, on whiteboards, self-checked, not recorded.
- Derive facts from strategies first (double 2s to get 4s; 5s as half of 10s), then build automatic recall with short, spaced practice.
- Personal fact goals ("my next 3 facts") rather than class speed leaderboards.

## 6. Topic-specific evidence and common Year 3 misconceptions

Syllabus wording below is quoted from the NESA *Mathematics K–10 Syllabus (2022)* on curriculum.nsw.edu.au (verified). Note that some focus areas are split into Part A and Part B, and Part B is often taught in Year 4.

### 6.1 Fractions as lengths on the number line

**Syllabus.** MA2-PF-01: "represents and compares halves, quarters, thirds and fifths as lengths on a number line and their related fractions formed by halving (eighths, sixths and tenths)". Part A includes creating thirds and fifths of a length "using techniques other than repeated halving", representing unit fractions and their multiples to a complete whole on a number line, complementary parts ("how much more to make one whole"), and **recreating the whole from a part** (reversible reasoning). Part B includes equivalence as lengths, the need for **equal wholes** to compare, fractions **equal to and greater than one**, renaming 4 quarters etc. as one whole, and locating 1/4 and 1/2 on lines that extend past 1.

**Evidence.**
- Clarke, Roche and Mitchell (2008), Australian research published in *Mathematics Teaching in the Middle School* (verified): treat fractions as **numbers** on number lines; use a definition that works for improper fractions (the denominator names the size of the part and the numerator counts the parts, so 7/3 is "seven thirds"); introduce **improper fractions and equivalence early**; use a variety of models (students are "model poor" and over-rely on circles); use benchmarks (0, 1/2, 1) and residual thinking.
- WWC *Developing Effective Fractions Instruction K–8* (Siegler et al., 2010) **[International]** (verified): "help students see that fractions are numbers that extend the number system" has **moderate** evidence.
- Fuchs et al. (2016) **[International]** (verified): in an intervention for at-risk Grade 4 students, most sessions focused on the measurement (number line) interpretation, and gains on NAEP fraction items were *mediated by* improvement in that interpretation.

**Common misconceptions.**
- Whole-number thinking: 1/8 > 1/4 "because 8 is bigger". Fix: fold strips and compare lengths of the same-size whole.
- Unequal parts still counted as fractions. Fix: non-examples on the number line.
- Counting tick marks instead of spaces; starting the count at the first mark rather than at 0.
- Thinking fractions only live between 0 and 1. Fix: count by quarters past 1 ("five quarters").
- "Gap thinking" (judging 5/6 and 7/8 equal because each is one short). Fix: residual thinking with lengths.
- Comparing fractions of different-sized wholes (the syllabus explicitly addresses this).

**Implications.** Use paper strips, then a number line drawn to the same length, then symbols. Do fifths and thirds by estimate-and-adjust (not halving). Ask reversal questions: "This strip is 1/3. Draw the whole."

### 6.2 Position, grid maps, directional language and compass points

**Syllabus** (Geometric measure A and B, verified). Part A: "Interpret movement on a map", "Orient a map to determine directions to travel", "**Use given directions to follow routes on land and Aboriginal maps without a grid reference system**", "Describe a route taken on a map using landmarks and directional language", and grid maps using row and column with "horizontal before vertical". Part B: create maps from an aerial view with grid references; "Use a given grid map and compass directions (N, S, E, W)"; "**Use natural resources or landmarks to identify north, south, east, west**"; "Relate compass directions to amounts of turn"; describe a return journey.

**Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander perspectives.** The syllabus places Aboriginal maps (which use landmarks, country, waterways and story rather than grids) alongside grid maps as a legitimate way of locating and describing routes. Using the sun's path, landmarks and natural features to find directions connects to Aboriginal navigation knowledge. Best practice is to use local Country with guidance from local Aboriginal community members or the school's AECG contact, rather than generic dot-art "maps". I found no specific outcome study here; this rests on the syllabus and on cultural protocol, not trial evidence. Torres Strait Islander perspectives are not named in the Stage 2 content I checked.

**Common misconceptions.** Reading grid references vertical-first; labelling the spaces versus the lines inconsistently; left and right flipping when the map is not oriented to the body (hence "orient a map"); treating "up the page" as always north; reversing directions on a return journey (N becomes S, left becomes right).

**Implications.** Start with whole-body movement (playground routes, giving instructions), then a classroom aerial map, then a grid. Link quarter turns to compass points (from N, a quarter turn clockwise faces E). This connects to angles (6.3).

### 6.3 Angles as turn, right angles, comparing angles

**Syllabus** (Geometric measure B, verified): compare angles to a right angle informally; describe angles as less than, equal to or greater than a right angle; describe angles in quarter turns up to a revolution; "identify the arms and vertex of an angle where one arm is visible and the other arm is invisible".

**Evidence.** Mitchelmore and White (Macquarie University and ACU; MERGA, verified) found children's recognition of angles depends on **how many arms are visible**. With two visible arms (scissors, corners, junctions) matching was correct 80 to 100% of the time. With one visible arm (slopes) it was about 40%, and with no visible arm (turns, a wheel) about 20%. Only 18% modelled a wheel's turn as an opening angle. Implication: start from two-arm physical angles, then move to turns, explicitly marking the "invisible" arm (the starting position). **Evidence strength: moderate** (careful descriptive research, consistent across their studies).

**Common misconceptions.** Thinking longer arms make a bigger angle; thinking a right angle must have one horizontal arm ("tilted" right angles are not seen as right angles); not seeing a turn as an angle; confusing "right" angle with right-hand direction.

**Implications.** Make a right-angle tester (folded paper corner) and hunt for angles around the room; draw angles with different arm lengths and the same opening; physically turn and trace the path with a rope or chalk to show the invisible arm.

### 6.4 Capacity and volume (L, mL, cm³)

**Syllabus.** MA2-3DS-02: "estimates, measures and compares capacities (internal volumes) using litres, millilitres and volumes using cubic centimetres" (verified). Content includes recognising the need for formal units, measuring to the nearest litre, recognising that one-litre containers come in many shapes, estimating then checking, and building rectangular prisms from cubic-centimetre blocks described in **layers**.

**Evidence.** Curry and Outhred (Macquarie, MERGA 2005, verified) interviewed 96 Sydney students in Years 1 to 4. **Filling** (pouring, iterating a cup) developed alongside length and was far easier than **packing** (cubes in a box). Packing was the hardest attribute even in Year 4, and success depended strongly on understanding area structure (rows and columns). Successful older students pictured layers and multiplied. **Evidence strength: moderate** (one well-designed Australian interview study, consistent with international work).

**Common misconceptions.** Taller means holds more (conservation); a 1 L container must look like a milk bottle; counting only visible cubes on a box's faces; confusing mL and L scale readings; thinking volume and capacity are unrelated.

**Implications.** Pour between different-shaped 1 L containers; estimate before measuring; build prisms layer by layer and say "3 rows of 4 is one layer, 2 layers"; link 1 cm³ to 1 mL only with a concrete demonstration (a 10 cm cube = 1 L can come later).

### 6.5 Money: dollars and cents, change, addition and subtraction

**Syllabus.** Additive relations A (verified): "Represent money values in multiple ways", "Recognise the relationship between dollars and cents", "Represent equivalent amounts of money using different denominations", "Perform calculations with money, including finding change". Part B: apply addition and subtraction to money and budgeting, decide whether a money problem needs addition or subtraction, and estimate to check.

**Evidence.** There is little Australian research on teaching money specifically. Jameson's review *Use of Money as a Decimal Representation* (Cambridge Mathematics, c. 2016) **[International]** (verified) found that money draws on informal knowledge but children often treat dollars and cents as **two separate whole numbers**. Success with money does not show decimal understanding. Steinle's "money thinking" (ordering to two places correctly, failing beyond cents) is a related Australian finding. **Evidence strength: weak to moderate.**

**Common misconceptions.** $1.5 read as "one dollar five"; adding cents past 100 without regrouping ($2.80 + $0.50 = $2.130); thinking the biggest coin is worth the most (the $1 coin is bigger than the $2 coin in Australia; the 50c coin is the biggest); subtracting change in the wrong direction; "change" understood only as counting coins.

**Implications.** Use the "counting up" (shopkeeper's) strategy for change on an empty number line; record money in standard form from the start; use real or realistic coins for problem solving but don't treat money as proof of decimal understanding.

### 6.6 Rounding to the nearest 10, 100, 1000

**Syllabus.** The 2022 syllabus frames this as *identifying the nearest* ten, hundred or thousand, not as a procedure. Stage 1 B: "Identify the nearest hundred to a number" (verified). Stage 2 Representing numbers B: "Identify the nearest thousand, 10 thousand or 100 thousand to numbers" (verified). Additive relations B uses estimation to check answers. I could not find the word "round" in the Stage 2 content I checked.

**Evidence.** I found no Australian or international trial on how best to teach rounding. **Evidence strength: weak**; the guidance follows from number line and place value research. Common errors include applying the "5 or more" rule to the wrong digit, changing all lower digits wrongly (e.g. 3482 to the nearest hundred becomes 3500 but written 3580), not knowing which two benchmarks a number lies between, and treating rounding as a rule rather than "which is closer?".

**Implications.** Always locate the number on a partly-labelled number line between two benchmarks (3400 and 3500), find the halfway point, and decide which is nearer. Only then introduce the halfway convention for exactly-midway numbers. Use rounding purposefully, for estimating answers to check algorithms and money totals.

### 6.7 Addition and subtraction algorithms with regrouping

**Syllabus.** Additive relations B (verified): "Model addition with and without regrouping and record the method used"; "**Use an algorithm with understanding** ... where efficient, involving 3-digit numbers"; "Recognise how hundreds are exchanged in subtraction algorithms requiring regrouping"; "**Recognise when mental strategies would be more efficient** than a vertical algorithm". Place value A (verified): "Regroup numbers flexibly, recognising one thousand as 10 hundreds and one hundred as 10 tens or 100 ones".

**Why place value must come first.** Siemon (MAV 2024, verified) puts place value as the second Big Idea: students need "10 of these is 1 of those" and to **rename** numbers (367 as 36 tens and 7 ones). Without renaming, "borrowing" is a meaningless rule. The EEF **[International]** recommends teaching procedures with understanding and teaching students to choose between strategies.

**Common misconceptions.** Subtracting the smaller digit from the larger in each column (52 − 37 = 25); regrouping when it is not needed; problems with zeros (403 − 158); not lining up place values; forgetting to add the regrouped ten.

**Implications.** Follow a deliberate sequence: base-10 blocks with physical trading, then a place value chart, then the algorithm recorded side by side with the materials. Practise renaming (403 as 39 tens and 13 ones) *before* the algorithm. Keep asking "Would a mental strategy be quicker?" (e.g. 300 − 198).

### 6.8 Decimals: tenths, hundredths and links to fractions

**Syllabus.** MA2-RN-02 (Representing numbers B, verified): divide a whole into 10 equal parts; the decimal point marks the position of the ones digit; 10 tenths is recorded as 1.0; represent and compare tenths using linear representations; subdivide tenths into hundredths; express decimals as tenths **and** hundredths; locate decimals on a number line; interpret trailing zeros; connect decimal strips to a number line; make connections between fractions and decimals for benchmark values.

**Evidence.** Steinle and Stacey (University of Melbourne, MERGA 2004, verified) followed students over several years. In primary grades, **71% showed "longer is larger" thinking** (e.g. 0.125 > 0.4), and many showed "shorter is larger". Normal teaching did not reliably fix these misconceptions, and passing simple tests overstated understanding. Their recommendations: teach that 3 tenths = 30 hundredths (not as separate topics), use the number line, and use diagnostic items (e.g. 0.45 vs 0.453). **Evidence strength: strong** (large longitudinal Australian dataset).

**Implications.** Use length models (metre ruler or decimal strips) linked to the fraction number line from 6.1. Say "three tenths", not "point three", at first. Include comparison items designed to expose longer-is-larger thinking in every hinge question set.

## 7. Maths anxiety, growth mindset and productive struggle

**Maths anxiety.** The Victorian DE page *Overcoming maths anxiety: practice* (verified) recommends: find out how students feel (e.g. maths metaphors, "maths is like ..."); **bibliotherapy** with picture books for younger students, followed by discussion; short breathing exercises and expressive writing for older students; and changing how adults talk about maths. The EEF **[International]** notes that attitudes decline with age (17% of Year 5 and 48% of Year 9 pupils reported not liking maths). It says anxiety should be recognised and addressed, but evidence on *how* to improve attitudes is limited. **Evidence strength: the link between anxiety and lower performance is well established; evidence for specific classroom interventions is weak to emerging.**

**Growth mindset.** Be cautious. Macnamara and Burgoyne (2023, *Psychological Bulletin*) **[International]** (verified) found only a small overall effect of growth mindset interventions on achievement. There was no significant effect in studies that actually changed mindsets or met best-practice design standards; 94% of samples did not isolate the treatment, and positive results were more common where authors had a financial interest. Others (e.g. Tipton et al., 2023) argue effects exist for some subgroups, but the classroom implication is the same: **posters and "brain talks" alone are unlikely to lift maths achievement**. The more defensible version is structural: tasks where effort visibly pays off, feedback on strategies, errors treated as information, and no fixed ability labels (which links back to the grouping evidence in section 3).

**Productive struggle.** Clarke et al. (2014, verified) found that teachers who used challenging tasks learned to hold back from telling, give more think time, and name the "zone of confusion" with students. Students sometimes resisted at first. Attard (verified) stresses that students also need "to experience success and a sense of achievement". The balance: struggle must be *productive*. This means the task is within reach with effort, an enabling prompt is ready, and the lesson ends with Discuss and connect so the struggle is resolved. CESE's expertise reversal point is the counterweight: novices on genuinely new content need explicit modelling first. **Evidence strength: moderate**, with the caution that unguided discovery for novices is not supported.

**Classroom implications**

- Use the language of "not yet" and "zone of confusion", but back it with structure: think time, then enabling prompt, then teacher conference.
- Avoid public speed tests and ranking. Praise strategies and persistence, not being "smart".
- Read a maths picture book and talk about the character's feelings once or twice a term. This is cheap and low risk, though the evidence is limited.

## 8. Formative assessment in maths

**Evidence**

- *What Works Best 2025 practical guide: Assessment* (CESE, Dec 2025, verified) describes **hinge questions**: pre-planned questions every student answers quickly (finger voting, **mini whiteboards**, flashcards) that decide whether to move on, reteach or practise. They need plausible distractors so students can't get the right answer for the wrong reason (citing Wiliam, 2015). It also recommends think-pair-share so all students respond, pausing for thinking time, observation, quick quizzes and traffic-light cards.
- *At a glance: Formative assessment* (AERO, July 2026, verified) recommends simple low-stakes checks, "quick quizzes, **exit tickets** or asking probing questions", used *during* learning and acted on (adjust teaching, catch misconceptions early). AERO also has a *Formative assessment in maths* video and facilitation guide (Dec 2022).
- What Works Best 2020 (verified): learning intentions, success criteria, feedback, peer and self assessment.
- EEF **[International]**: assessment should find out what pupils do and don't know; feedback should be specific, sparing and focused on next steps; anticipate misconceptions.
- Clarke, Roche and Mitchell (2008) and Siemon's targeted teaching model both support short **one-to-one or diagnostic interviews** for students whose written work is hard to interpret.

**Evidence strength: strong** for formative assessment as a practice; individual techniques (hinge questions, exit tickets) are well supported in practice but rarely tested on their own.

**Classroom implications for Year 3**

- Write one hinge question per lesson where each wrong answer points to a known misconception from section 6. Example: "Which is bigger? A 0.7  B 0.65  C They're equal" (B points to longer-is-larger thinking).
- Use mini whiteboards in every *we do*; scan, then decide whether to release students or reteach.
- Use a 2 to 3 question exit ticket to form the next day's teacher-table group.
- Use quick pre-tests at the start of a unit to plan enabling and extending prompts, not to set fixed groups.

### Gaps and caveats

- AERO has no maths-specific practice guide on fractions, measurement, geometry or maths anxiety as of October 2026; its maths content is mainly general guides plus explicit instruction and formative assessment videos.
- Rounding, money and position have little direct outcome research at any level. Guidance there is derived from place value, number line and decimal research.
- Several NESA syllabus pages render with missing words online ("Loading"). Quotes were taken only from intact text; check the full wording on the syllabus site before putting it in programs.
- The Victorian toolkit site is moving to Arc; URLs may change.
